# Debts still open at v1.1

**One row carries over from v1 — #4 — nine are new, one of the nine closed between being
written and being committed** (#11, and it is left in place with what closed it), **and one was
ruled rather than paid** (#16: keep the behaviour, reason ADR 0083). So the count of debts a
reader has to act on is eight, and the table below has ten rows: a register that deletes a settled
row is a register that gets the same question asked again.

> **A row written from a gate log is one landing behind by construction, and three rows proved it
> in one night.** #4 said *"needs a PD-held graph, owner pdha"* and the measurement said the lock
> was missing rather than the graph; §J11 of `phase-16-mpp.md` said the join rewrite was *left* and
> `git log -S` found it built four days earlier, by a commit whose own message recorded the
> permission that section was still asking for; #10 asked for a fix that two commits had already
> landed, citing a gate log older than both. Each cost a unit being issued — #10's to two lanes at
> once. **So: before a row is acted on, re-read it against `git log` for the file it names.** One
> command, and it is cheaper than the unit.

**Then #17 was paid and two rows were opened by paying it** (#19, #20 — both found by measuring
the shapes rather than by reading the code, which is the argument for measuring), so the count is
eleven to act on across thirteen rows (#21 and #22 arrived the same way, from the same corpus; #22
was #12's consequence until b4's int4 ladder landed and it stayed). **#21 and #22 were then paid, and measuring each
first found a live defect in the thing it was said to be missing** — #21 two, in the `= ANY` that
already existed, and #22 a `22003` on a statement a real server answers. Paying #22 also opened
three rows (#23, #24, #25), because the measurement said the one row was three things: so the count kept moving on both sides. **Where it stands: §1 has five rows and all five are open** — #15, #19, #35, #36, #37 — and §2 holds twenty-four that are done. #33 closed the day it was written, by the method note above it: the second reader was found by asking who else decides the same thing. #35 and #36 came out of groups 5 and 6 the way #26 and #27 came out of #19: the measurement that closed a type also said what the *next* thing about it would cost. (#35 was written as #34 and renumbered, h1 having claimed that number first — the register's own rule, and the second time it has been applied this week.) #26 and #27 arrived from #19's own family and were paid the same night, which is the pattern this register keeps producing: a measurement finds more than the row it was taken for, and most of what it finds is smaller than the row, with #11, #16, #17, #21 and #22 settled. A register that grows
while debts are paid is a register that was wrong about the size, which is the whole reason each
row now says how it was measured. #17 moved to §2 with what closed it, which is this file's own
rule: close a row where the row lives, in the commit that closes it. The numbering continues
[`debts-v1.md`](debts-v1.md)'s rather than restarting, because the numbers are quoted across lanes
and a register that renumbers makes every quotation of it wrong. A closed row leaves a **gap**.

**A third rule, and it is about where a defect hides rather than about this file.** Twice in one
week a bug turned out to be **two readers of one fact**, disagreeing because one of them was
written later and nobody joined them up: `parse::lower`'s constant *fold* decided which casts exist
without ever asking `pg_catalog::casts_to`, which is the gate every cast over an *expression* goes
through — so `'101'::bit(3)::int2` answered `101` where the same pair through a column was `42846`
— and g1's `BETWEEN` splitter read a bound the deparser had already re-parenthesised, which is the
same shape one layer up. Neither is visible from either reader alone; both are visible the moment
somebody asks *who else answers this question*. **So when a row below names a wrong answer, grep
for the second site that decides the same thing before sizing it** — three of the fixes that came
out of this register were one line once the pair was found, and none of them was findable from the
site the symptom appeared at.

Two rules come from that file's own failures, and both are applied here rather than restated:

> **Close a row where the row lives, in the commit that closes it.** Every lag `debts-v1.md` had
> came from a closure recorded somewhere the register does not read — once in an ADR whose first
> line said it closed two rows of it.
>
> **Asking "is the site still there" is not asking "is the symptom still there."** Six of that
> file's eight rows turned out to be closed when somebody looked. So every row below was put to the
> tree or to a log before it was written, and each says which; the two that could only be taken
> from another lane's evidence say that too.
>
> **A reader nobody wrote for is invisible to a register that reads code.** Six readers of a
> stored expression had a writer that shaped the text they print; the seventh — an exclusion
> constraint's predicate — had none, so `pg_get_indexdef` on the index behind it printed whatever
> the user typed, and no row here said so because nothing in the tree looks wrong: the writer that
> is missing has no site to inspect. It took a cross product to name it. The same shape put
> [ADR 0090](../adr/0090-a-stored-expression-is-deparsed-by-the-statement-that-writes-it.md)'s own
> decision — *every reader returns those bytes unchanged* — in the odd position of being **an
> aspiration its file recorded as a fact** for as long as two of its readers were re-parenthesising
> on the way out. A decision record is checkable: walk its sentence over every caller, not over the
> ones it lists.
>
> **The direction where this node is *more permissive* than PostgreSQL had never been asked by a
> corpus.** Every capture here was taken by writing what a real server accepts and comparing the
> answers, so a row could only be opened where the node refused or answered differently — and a

> a reader that was measured on the shapes that happened to reach it is **wrong and green**, and
> stays that way until something asks it the cross product. Both are the same rule — *the corpus
> can only answer the question it was written to ask* — and both are cheapest to fix by writing
> the enumeration rather than the example.
